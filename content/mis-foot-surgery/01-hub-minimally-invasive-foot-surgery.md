## H1: Minimally Invasive Foot Surgery

*(Answer-first intro)*

Minimally invasive foot surgery corrects foot problems — like bunions and
hammertoes — through very small openings in the skin instead of long incisions.
Using specialized instruments and live X-ray guidance, the surgeon works on the
bone directly while disturbing less of the surrounding tissue. For the right
patient, that can mean smaller scars and an easier early recovery.

## H2: What "minimally invasive" actually means

You may see it called keyhole surgery, percutaneous surgery, or MIS. They all
describe the same idea: instead of opening the foot to see the bone, the surgeon
reaches it through small openings — often less than a centimeter — and watches
their work on a live X-ray screen. The correction to the bone is just as real;
the path to it is smaller. [DR. LEFF: confirm.]

## H2: The honest trade-offs

Minimally invasive surgery is a tool, not a miracle. Two things are true at once:

1. **The benefits are real.** Less tissue disruption, smaller scars, and — for
   many patients — earlier walking in a protective shoe. [VERIFY + citation]
2. **It isn't for every foot.** Severe deformity, arthritis in the joint, or poor
   bone quality can make a traditional approach the safer, better choice. Bone
   still takes months to fully heal either way.

Our promise is simple: we'll recommend the procedure your foot needs — and if
that's not a minimally invasive one, or not surgery at all, we'll say so.

## H2: Frequently asked questions

**Is minimally invasive surgery the same as laser surgery?**
No. Despite the marketing you may see elsewhere, these procedures use precise
surgical instruments under X-ray guidance — not lasers. [DR. LEFF: confirm.]

**Will my insurance cover it?**
Minimally invasive procedures are generally covered the same way as their
traditional versions when medically necessary. We verify your benefits before
scheduling.

**How do I know if I'm a candidate?**
Only an exam and X-rays can answer that. If you have a bunion or hammertoe that
still hurts despite roomier shoes, pads, or orthotics, that's the right time to
be evaluated.

**How should I choose a surgeon?**
Ask how often they perform the specific procedure, how they decide who's a
candidate, and what their plan is if your foot needs a different approach. You
should hear clear answers to all three — from us or from anyone.
