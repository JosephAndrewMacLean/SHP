## H2: What happens during the procedure

A bunion is a bony bump at the base of the big toe that forms when the bones of
the forefoot shift out of line. Surgery — any bunion surgery — works by moving
those bones back into position.

In the minimally invasive approach:

- The surgeon makes one or more small openings, often less than a centimeter each.
  [DR. LEFF: confirm typical incision size/count for your technique.]
- A small burr (a precise surgical instrument) reshapes and repositions the bone
  through those openings, guided by live X-ray.
- The corrected bone is usually held in place with small screws while it heals.
  [DR. LEFF: confirm fixation method.]
- The procedure is typically done as outpatient surgery — you go home the same
  day. [VERIFY location: Synergy Surgery Center?]

## H2: Minimally invasive vs. traditional bunion surgery

Both approaches correct the bunion. The difference is how the surgeon reaches the
bone. *(Editor: render as a simple two-column table.)*

| | Minimally invasive | Traditional (open) |
|---|---|---|
| Incision | One or more small openings, typically under 1 cm [VERIFY] | One longer incision over the joint |
| Soft tissue | Less disruption to surrounding tissue | More exposure of the joint |
| Early recovery | Often allows earlier walking in a protective shoe [VERIFY + citation] | Typically more restricted early on |
| Scarring | Smaller marks | Longer scar |
| Best for | Mild-to-moderate bunions in appropriate candidates [DR. LEFF: confirm] | A wider range, including severe deformity or arthritis in the joint |

Neither approach is "better" for everyone. The honest answer — and the one that
matters — is which approach fits *your* foot. That's what the exam is for.

## H2: Who is a good candidate — and who isn't

Minimally invasive bunion surgery is not right for every bunion. In general, the
best candidates have: [DR. LEFF: review/replace this list]

- A mild to moderate bunion that still hurts despite wider shoes, pads, or
  orthotics
- Good bone quality and no significant arthritis in the big-toe joint
- Realistic expectations about healing time — the bone still needs months to fully
  heal, even when the incisions are small

You may be steered toward a different procedure if your bunion is severe, if the
joint is arthritic, or if past surgery has changed the anatomy. If minimally
invasive surgery isn't the right tool for your foot, we'll tell you — and explain
what is.

## H2: Frequently asked questions

*(Editor: this block gets FAQPage schema. Questions mirror real searches.)*

**What is minimally invasive bunion surgery?**
It's bunion correction performed through one or more very small openings instead
of a long incision. The surgeon realigns the bone using specialized instruments
and live X-ray guidance, and the bone is typically secured with small screws while
it heals. [DR. LEFF: confirm.]

**Is minimally invasive bunion surgery covered by insurance?**
Usually yes, when it's medically necessary — the same standard as traditional
bunion surgery. Every plan differs, so we verify your specific benefits before
scheduling.

**Who is a candidate?**
Most candidates have a mild to moderate bunion that still hurts despite
non-surgical care, healthy bone, and no significant arthritis in the joint. An
exam and X-rays are the only way to know for sure. [DR. LEFF: confirm.]

**Does it work as well as traditional surgery?**
For appropriately selected patients, published studies report correction and
satisfaction comparable to traditional techniques. [Content team: add citation
before publish — this claim cannot ship unsourced.] Choosing the right procedure
for the right foot is what drives a good result.

**How long does the surgery take?**
Typically about an hour or less, done as outpatient surgery. [VERIFY]

**How long is recovery?**
Many patients walk in a protective shoe early on, but full bone healing takes
roughly 6–12 weeks, and swelling can take longer to settle completely. [VERIFY]

**Is it safe?**
All surgery carries risks — including infection, nerve irritation, delayed bone
healing, or the bunion returning over time. Your surgeon will go through your
personal risks at your consultation. [DR. LEFF: confirm risk list.]
