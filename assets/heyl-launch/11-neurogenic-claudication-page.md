## Page copy — DRAFT FOR PHYSICIAN REVIEW

### H1
> Leg pain when you walk: understanding neurogenic claudication

### Opening — direct answer, snippet-eligible

> **Neurogenic claudication is leg pain, heaviness, cramping, numbness or weakness that comes on
> when you walk or stand and eases when you sit down or lean forward. It's usually caused by
> narrowing of the spinal canal in the lower back, which puts pressure on the nerves that travel to
> your legs.**
>
> If you've noticed you can walk further leaning on a shopping cart than standing upright, or that
> your legs feel fine until you've been on them a few minutes, this is a pattern worth having
> evaluated.

*(Note for editor: the paragraph above is written at 55 words to be eligible for a featured snippet.
Keep it as the first thing under the H1. Don't add an introduction above it.)*

### What it feels like

> People describe it differently, but the pattern is consistent:
>
> - Pain, heaviness, cramping or burning in the buttocks, thighs or calves
> - Numbness or tingling down one or both legs
> - Legs that feel weak or unreliable after walking a certain distance
> - Symptoms that start after a few minutes of walking or standing
> - **Relief when you sit down, bend forward, or lean on something**
>
> That last point is the most telling one. Bending forward opens up the space around the nerves, so
> people often find they can push a shopping cart or ride a bike comfortably while walking upright
> the same distance is difficult.
>
> Symptoms often affect both legs, though not always equally, and they can vary day to day.

### What causes it

> The usual cause is **lumbar spinal stenosis** — narrowing of the canal in the lower spine that the
> nerves pass through.
>
> That narrowing typically develops gradually with age. Discs lose height, the small joints at the
> back of the spine enlarge with arthritis, and the ligaments inside the canal thicken. None of these
> is unusual in itself. Together, they can leave less room than the nerves need — particularly when
> you stand upright, which narrows the space further.
>
> That's why standing and walking bring symptoms on, and why bending forward relieves them.
>
> Less commonly, narrowing can follow a disc herniation, a spinal fracture, or previous spine surgery.

### Neurogenic or vascular? — the distinction that changes everything

> There's a second condition that causes leg pain when walking: **vascular claudication**, where
> narrowed arteries limit blood flow to the leg muscles. The symptoms overlap enough that the two are
> regularly confused, but the causes are completely different — one is a nerve problem, the other is
> a circulation problem — and so are the treatments.
>
> Some differences that tend to separate them:
>
> | | Neurogenic (nerve) | Vascular (circulation) |
> |---|---|---|
> | What relieves it | Sitting or leaning forward — position matters | Stopping, in any position |
> | Walking uphill | Often easier — you're leaning forward | Often harder — muscles need more blood |
> | Riding a bike | Usually comfortable — you're bent forward | Usually brings symptoms on |
> | Other symptoms | Numbness, tingling, back pain | Cold feet, weak pulses, skin or nail changes |
> | Time to relief | Can take several minutes | Usually quicker |
>
> **These are patterns, not a diagnosis.** People can have both conditions at once, and either can
> present unusually. This distinction is one your physician should make with an examination and
> testing — but noticing which pattern fits you is genuinely useful information to bring to an
> appointment.

### How it's diagnosed

> Diagnosis starts with your history and a physical examination — often the most informative part,
> because the pattern of what brings symptoms on and what relieves them is distinctive.
>
> From there, your physician may use:
>
> - **MRI** — shows the spinal canal and how much room the nerves have
> - **X-ray** — shows alignment and can show movement between vertebrae
> - **EMG and nerve conduction studies** — electrical tests that show whether nerves are actually
>   being affected and where. These are particularly useful when it isn't clear whether symptoms are
>   coming from the spine, from a nerve further down the leg, or from a general neuropathy.
> - **Circulation testing** — to check for or rule out a vascular cause
>
> One thing worth knowing: **narrowing on a scan doesn't automatically explain your symptoms.** Some
> degree of narrowing is common with age and causes no trouble at all. What matters is whether the
> narrowing found on imaging matches the symptoms you're actually having.

### How it's treated

> Treatment usually moves in steps, starting with the least invasive options.
>
> **Non-surgical care, usually first:**
>
> - **Physical therapy** — targeted exercise, often including flexion-based movement, plus core and
>   hip strengthening and walking tolerance work
> - **Activity adjustments** — pacing, using support while walking, changing how you approach
>   distances
> - **Medication** — to manage symptoms while other treatments take effect
>
> **Interventional options:**
>
> - **Epidural steroid injections** — reduce inflammation around the compressed nerves. Relief
>   varies from person to person and isn't permanent, but it can create room to make real progress in
>   therapy, and it helps confirm the source of the pain.
> - **Other targeted injections** — where a specific structure appears to be contributing
>
> **Surgery** is considered when symptoms are limiting and haven't responded to conservative care,
> or when there's progressive weakness or a neurological change. A decompression procedure creates
> more space for the nerves.
>
> Most people don't need surgery. But if you do, it helps to be in a practice where that
> conversation can happen without starting over somewhere new.

### When to seek care right away

> Some symptoms need urgent evaluation rather than a routine appointment:
>
> - **Loss of bladder or bowel control**
> - **Numbness in the groin or inner thighs**
> - **Weakness that's rapidly getting worse**
> - Severe pain following a fall or injury
>
> These are uncommon, but they can indicate serious nerve compression that needs prompt attention.
> **Go to an emergency department or call your physician immediately** — don't wait for a scheduled
> appointment.

### Common questions

> **Does neurogenic claudication go away on its own?**
> Symptoms often fluctuate, with better and worse periods. The underlying narrowing generally doesn't
> reverse on its own, but many people manage symptoms well for years with therapy, activity changes,
> and injections when needed.
>
> **Is neurogenic claudication the same as sciatica?**
> They're related but not identical. Sciatica is pain following the sciatic nerve, usually down one
> leg, and it's often present at rest. Neurogenic claudication is more typically brought on by
> walking or standing, frequently affects both legs, and eases when you sit or lean forward. Both
> involve nerve compression in the lower back, and someone can have both.
>
> **Will I need surgery?**
> Most people don't. Conservative treatment and injections manage symptoms for many patients.
> Surgery is generally considered when symptoms significantly limit daily life despite other
> treatment, or when there's progressive weakness.
>
> **How serious is it?**
> For most people it's a quality-of-life problem rather than a dangerous one — it limits how far you
> can walk and what you can do. It's worth evaluating, both because treatment can help and because
> your physician should confirm what's actually causing the symptoms.
>
> **What kind of doctor treats this?**
> Pain medicine physicians, physiatrists (rehabilitation physicians), and spine surgeons all treat
> it. Which is right depends on your situation. A pain medicine physician or physiatrist is a
> reasonable starting point if you haven't had a surgical evaluation.
