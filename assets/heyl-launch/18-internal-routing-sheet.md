# Internal routing — when to send a patient to Dr. Heyl

**For:** SHP physicians, PAs, physical therapists, chiropractors, and schedulers
**Status:** DRAFT — **requires clinical leadership review.** This document routes patients; it can't
go out on marketing's say-so.
**Owner:** Randall (drafting) · **Approver:** Clinical leadership · **Live by:** Sept 1

---

## Why this exists

Dr. Heyl is added capacity. If he only takes patients who would otherwise have seen Dr. Oddo,
Dr. Lee, Dr. Kassa or Dr. Singh, the practice has gained nothing — we've moved volume, not grown it.

This sheet exists to send him the patients who are genuinely his, and to give everyone a clear answer
to "who should this person see?"

---

## Send to Dr. Heyl

**Peripheral nerve problems** — his primary focus
- Suspected mononeuropathy or focal nerve entrapment
- Nerve pain following a specific path in an arm or leg
- Numbness, tingling or burning in a defined distribution
- Nerve pain after surgery or injury
- Complex regional pain syndrome
- Patients who might be candidates for a peripheral nerve block or nerve stimulation

**Anyone needing EMG or nerve conduction testing**
- Is the weakness from the nerve, or from pain? *(He's specifically interested in this distinction.)*
- Is this a pinched nerve in the spine, or a nerve problem further down the limb?
- Confirming or excluding a suspected neuropathy
- Following a known nerve problem over time

**Spine pain, non-surgical**
- Persistent axial back or neck pain after conservative care
- Radicular pain — sciatica, cervical radiculopathy
- Spinal stenosis and leg pain with walking
- Sacroiliac joint pain
- Patients who may benefit from a diagnostic or therapeutic injection

**Peripheral joint pain**
- Knee, hip, shoulder pain that hasn't responded to therapy
- Arthritis pain in patients who aren't surgical candidates, aren't ready, or still hurt after
  replacement
- Candidates for genicular nerve block or joint injection

**From Livonia specifically:** appropriate back and neck pain overflow can route directly to him.

## Don't send to Dr. Heyl

Not a judgment about these patients — they need a different pathway, and sending them to the wrong
door delays their care.

| Instead of Dr. Heyl | Where it should go |
|---|---|
| Widespread or centralized pain | Broader pain pathway — review first |
| Fibromyalgia | Broader pain pathway |
| Heavy chronic opioid management | Review first — not direct procedural scheduling |
| Cancer-related pain | Not part of his practice |
| Patients wanting kyphoplasty | Another provider |
| Patients wanting PRP or stem cell therapy | Not available from him at launch |
| Progressive weakness, or a neurological deficit that's worsening | **Spine surgeon, urgently** |
| Suspected cauda equina — bladder/bowel change, saddle numbness | **Emergency evaluation now** |
| Fracture or instability | Spine surgeon |
| Patients wanting an injection with no evaluation | Book an evaluation — he won't promise a procedure in advance |

## Dr. Heyl or one of our other pain physicians?

We have five pain physicians from September. There's real overlap, and that's fine — but here's the
distinction worth knowing:

**Lean toward Dr. Heyl when:**
- A **peripheral nerve** is the suspected problem
- **EMG or nerve conduction testing** is needed
- The question is diagnostic — *what is actually generating this pain?*
- Weakness needs sorting out: nerve damage or pain-limited?
- The patient has a specific **functional goal** and wants a plan built around it
- Peripheral nerve stimulation may be worth considering
- The patient is at **Livonia (Mon/Tue)** or **Sterling Heights (Wed)** and access matters

**Stay with the existing team when:**
- The patient already has an established relationship — continuity wins
- It's a straightforward repeat injection in an existing plan
- It's outside his locations or days

**When in doubt, either is reasonable.** They're colleagues in one practice. Don't leave a patient
waiting while this gets decided.

## What to include in a referral

Sending this makes his evaluation faster and better:

- **What the patient can't do** — the functional problem, not just the pain score
- How long it's been going on
- What's already been tried — therapy, medications, injections, and what happened
- Any imaging, and the report
- Whether there's weakness, and whether it's changing
- Anything relevant about insurance or work status

He evaluates one clearly defined problem at a time. If someone has several pain sources, say which is
worst — he'll work through them in order.

## By service line

**Spine surgeons** — patients who don't need surgery yet, or don't want it; patients needing
diagnostic clarification before a surgical decision; post-operative patients with ongoing nerve pain.
Send back for surgical review any time; that pathway runs both ways.

**Orthopedics** — joint pain in non-surgical patients; patients who still hurt after joint
replacement; suspected nerve involvement in a limb.

**Hand** — suspected nerve entrapment needing EMG confirmation; nerve pain that isn't surgical.

**Foot and ankle** — suspected peripheral neuropathy or focal nerve pain in the foot; nerve pain
after foot surgery.

**Physical therapy** — patients plateauing in therapy where pain is limiting progress; patients where
it's unclear whether weakness is neurological or pain-driven. *PT often spots these first.*

**Chiropractic** — patients needing imaging or nerve testing beyond conservative care.

**Pain management colleagues** — peripheral nerve cases, EMG requests, or patients who could be
stimulation candidates.
