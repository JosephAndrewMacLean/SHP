   upstream of the phone.
3. **It doubles as referral collateral.** Referring offices face this question constantly.

It also demonstrates something we can't claim any other way: that we're willing to tell someone they
might not need surgery. That's the credibility our spine line currently has no way to show.

**Careful framing:** this must not read as "come to the pain doctor instead of the surgeon." Both are
our physicians. The article's value is that it's genuinely even-handed.

## Article copy — DRAFT FOR PHYSICIAN REVIEW

### H1
> Spine surgeon or pain management physician: which one should you see?

### Opening — direct answer

> **If you have back or neck pain and haven't had a specialist evaluation yet, a pain management
> physician or physiatrist is usually the better starting point. They diagnose the source of the
> pain and treat it without surgery. A spine surgeon is the right first call when there's a
> structural problem that needs surgical repair — progressive weakness, nerve damage, instability, or
> pain that hasn't responded to good non-surgical care.**
>
> Most people with back pain don't need surgery. But "most people" isn't everyone, and the point of
> an evaluation is to find out which you are.

### What each specialist actually does

> **A pain management physician** — often a physiatrist, sometimes an anesthesiologist by background —
> focuses on identifying which structure is generating your pain and treating it without an
> operation. That includes examination, imaging review, nerve testing, physical therapy direction,
> medication where appropriate, and image-guided procedures: injections, nerve blocks, radiofrequency
> ablation, and nerve stimulation.
>
> A physician trained in **Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation** brings a particular angle: that
> specialty is built around function and movement, so the question isn't only "where does it hurt"
> but "what is this stopping you from doing, and what would it take to get that back."
>
> **A spine surgeon** — an orthopedic spine surgeon or a neurosurgeon — treats structural problems
> that need surgical correction. Nerve compression that hasn't responded to other treatment,
> instability, deformity, fractures, and progressive neurological problems.
>
> Here's the part that surprises people: **most spine surgeons spend a great deal of their time
> telling patients they don't need surgery.** Seeing a surgeon isn't a commitment to an operation.

### When to start with a pain management physician

> - Back or neck pain that's persisted for weeks and isn't improving
> - Pain radiating into an arm or leg
> - Numbness or tingling without significant weakness
> - Leg pain when you walk that eases when you sit or lean forward
> - Joint pain that hasn't responded to therapy
> - You've had an MRI showing something, but nobody has explained whether it's what's causing your pain
> - You want to understand your options before considering surgery
> - You've been told you might need surgery and want a non-surgical opinion first

### When to start with a spine surgeon

> - **Progressive weakness** — a foot or hand that's getting weaker, not just painful
> - **A confirmed structural problem** that non-surgical treatment hasn't helped
> - A spine fracture, or significant instability or deformity
> - Nerve compression with worsening neurological signs
> - You've completed good conservative care — therapy, injections, time — and remain significantly
>   limited
> - A previous spine surgery that hasn't resolved the problem

### Symptoms that need care today, not an appointment

> Some symptoms need immediate evaluation regardless of who you'd normally see:
>
> - **Loss of bladder or bowel control**
> - **Numbness in the groin or inner thighs**
> - **Weakness that's rapidly worsening**
> - **Severe pain following a significant injury**
> - **Back pain with fever**, or back pain with a history of cancer
>
> **Go to an emergency department or call your physician now.** These are uncommon, but they can
> indicate problems where timing matters.

### What a first appointment looks like

> With either specialist, expect broadly the same shape:
>
> A conversation about your symptoms — where the pain is, what makes it better and worse, what it's
> stopping you doing. A physical examination testing strength, sensation, reflexes and movement. A
> review of any imaging you've had. A discussion of what they think is going on and what the next
> step is.
>
> **What you shouldn't expect is a procedure on the first visit.** A good evaluation comes before
> treatment, not after. If you're told you need an injection or an operation before anyone has
> examined you properly, that's worth a second opinion.
>
> It also helps to arrive with a clear sense of what you want to be able to do again — walk the dog,
> get through a shift, sleep through the night. A specific functional goal is more useful to a
> physician than a number on a pain scale.

### If you're not sure

> You don't have to get this right on your own.
>
> Your primary care physician can point you in a reasonable direction. So can a physical therapist,
> who often spots which patients need a specialist and which kind.
>
> And in a practice where both specialties work together, the entry point matters less. If you see a
> pain physician and turn out to need a surgical opinion, that's a conversation between colleagues,
> not a new referral and another three-week wait. The reverse is equally true — most people who see a
> spine surgeon leave with a non-surgical plan.

### Common questions

> **Do I need a referral?**
> It depends on your insurance. Some plans require a referral from your primary care physician;
> others don't. Our scheduling team can check when you call.
>
> **Will a pain management physician just give me an injection?**
> They shouldn't, not before examining you. An injection is one tool among several, and it's most
> useful when there's a clear target — sometimes to treat, sometimes to confirm where the pain is
> coming from. A first visit should be an evaluation.
>
> **Is seeing a surgeon a commitment to surgery?**
> No. Surgeons evaluate and frequently recommend against operating. A surgical consultation is a
> consultation.
>
> **What if I've already had an MRI?**
> Bring it, or the report. It's genuinely useful. Bear in mind that findings on a scan are common and
> don't always explain symptoms — part of the evaluation is working out whether what shows up is
> actually what's causing your pain.
>
> **What if I've already had spine surgery and still hurt?**
> That's a specific situation both specialties see regularly, and it's worth a proper evaluation
> rather than assuming nothing more can be done.
