# Medical Review Guide — Spine Content (Reviewers + Process)

**Purpose:** every clinical page in the spine program requires a named physician reviewer
before publish (blocking gate — `spine-content-cluster-map.md` §3, brand guardrails). This
file is the system of record for how that works. The surgeon-facing version (short, styled)
is published as the "Spine Content Review — Reviewer's Guide" artifact.

**Roles:** Randall routes drafts and tracks the queue · Joe recruits reviewers + owns SLA
escalations · Reviewers: 2 named physicians (1 surgical spine, 1 interventional/conservative —
names TBD by Joe) · Clinical accuracy is always the physician's call; marketing never
overrides a clinical objection.

---

## Why physician review (the two reasons we tell reviewers)

1. **Patient safety and trust.** These pages reach patients deciding what their symptoms mean
   and whether/where to seek care. Wrong or overstated content harms patients and the
   practice.
2. **The expertise is the ranking strategy.** Google and AI answer engines elevate medical
   content that demonstrates real clinician expertise (E-E-A-T) — a named reviewer, a review
   date, and first-hand clinical insight are exactly what separates our pages from the
   generic AI content flooding these topics. The reviewer's name + credentials publish on the
   page ("Medically reviewed by…"), with their permission.

## The review = 3 questions (keep it this simple for reviewers)

1. **Is anything clinically wrong, outdated, or overstated?** Mark it — a strikethrough and a
   margin note is enough; no need to rewrite prose.
2. **What would you add that only a practicing spine physician would know?** 2–3 short
   contributions per page ("what I tell my patients…", a misconception you correct weekly, a
   practical recovery nuance). Marketing formats them; this is the expertise boost.
3. **Would you put your name on this page?** If not — what has to change first?

## Pass 1 — accuracy checklist (blocking)

- Symptoms, mechanisms, and terminology correct; nothing missing that changes meaning.
- **Red flags / emergency guidance present and correct** (cauda equina signs, trauma,
  progressive neuro deficit → 911/ER language, not "book an appointment").
- Conservative-first pathway represented accurately (PT, injections before surgical framing
  where clinically appropriate).
- Surgical indications and candidacy statements accurate — not every reader is a candidate,
  and the page must not imply otherwise.
- Recovery timelines realistic (ranges, not promises).
- Any statistic has a citable source the reviewer accepts — otherwise it's cut. Never a stat
  placed so it reads as a guaranteed outcome (FTC).
- Procedure descriptions match how SHP actually performs them; techniques/technology claims
  match what our surgeons actually use.
- Credentials, specialties, and "who does this at SHP" statements accurate (esp. ACDR).

## Pass 2 — expertise boost (what lifts the page)

Ask for 2–3 of these per page, 2–3 sentences each (illustrative formats, not copy):
- **"What I tell my patients"** — the framing they actually use in clinic.
- **The misconception** they correct most often on this topic.
- **The practical detail** patients don't expect (about prep, recovery, return-to-activity).
- **What they look for** in exam/imaging that decides the pathway.
- **When they advise against** surgery — candor is a trust and E-E-A-T signal.

Rules for boosts: first-person is welcome (attributed), no patient stories/PHI, no guarantees,
no superiority claims ("best in Michigan" etc.), plain language preferred — marketing will
translate jargon but never alter clinical meaning without re-confirmation.

## What reviewers should NOT spend time on

- **Reading level.** Pages are deliberately written at Grade 6–8 (patients and AI engines both
  need this). Correct the *fact*, keep the plain language — don't re-medicalize the prose.
  If precision truly requires the technical term, keep it and we'll gloss it in parentheses.
- SEO, titles, links, formatting, grammar — marketing's job.
- House style/brand voice — marketing's job.

## Mechanics

- **Delivery:** one draft at a time (doc with comments enabled), subject line
  `[Spine review] <page> — due <date>`.
- **Effort:** condition/treatment page ≈ 15–20 min; learning-hub article ≈ 10 min.
  Expected load at full cadence ≈ 2–3 pages/week per reviewer (<1 hr/week).
- **SLA: 3 business days.** Randall nudges at day 3; Joe escalates at day 5. Drafting never
  stops while reviews are pending — publishing does.
- **Disagreement:** clinical objections win, full stop. If two reviewers disagree with each
  other, the page holds and Joe brokers a call.
- **Attestation (returned with each page):**
  - ☐ Approved as written · ☐ Approved with my corrections incorporated · ☐ Not approved — discuss
  - Corrections: (inline or listed)
  - Expertise contributions: (2–3)
  - Publish my name + credentials as reviewer: ☐ yes ☐ no
  - Name, credentials, specialty · Date
- **What publishes:** "Medically reviewed by [Name], [MD/DO], [Specialty] — [Month Year]" +
  link to their bio page; the review date; the page's educational disclaimer (review attests
  accuracy at time of review — it is not individual medical advice). Liability questions →
  practice leadership/legal, not marketing.
- **Re-review cadence:** any material clinical edit → re-review before republish; otherwise
  annual refresh (reviewer confirms or updates; date refreshes only with an actual review).
- **Tracking:** Randall logs each page's reviewer + status + date in `seo/change-log.md`
  alongside the publish batch. A page without a logged attestation does not publish.
